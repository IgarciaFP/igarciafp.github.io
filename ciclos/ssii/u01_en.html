1. Introduction and basic definitions

1.1. Hardware and Software

Computer architecture describes the parts that make up a computer. We can distinguish two basic kinds of components:

► Physical components, collectively known as hardware and which is made up of all the circuitry and electronic components of the computer.

► Logical components, collectively known as software and consisting of the intangible components of a computer: the programs that run on it and the information or data it processes. Without software, a computer would be a machine that does nothing.

Remember:

If we can touch it, it is hardware (physical component), if it is intangible, it is software (logical component).

1.2. Computer science and computers

The computer science is the science that studies the acquisition, representation, processing and transmission of information, automatically, using machines called computers.

The term information has a broad meaning in computing, and refers to a set of symbols that represent facts, objects or ideas.

The computer is a machine, generally electronic and digital, capable of accepting input data, carrying out a series of mathematical and logical operations with them and providing the resulting information through an output medium; all without human intervention, using a set of instructions, that is, a program previously stored in the computer's memory.

Did you know that...?

The Spanish term informática comes from the French informatique, coined by engineer Philippe Dreyfus in the early 1960s. The word combines information and automatique.

The concept of information is broader than that of a datum: a fact needed to understand something or draw conclusions, or information arranged for processing by a computer.

Remember:

A computer is an electronic and digital device that processes information following instructions (stored program).

Black box model.

In systems theory, a black box receives input data, processes it and produces output information. We can view a computer as a black box: for now, we do not need to examine its internal operation, only the data it receives and the useful information it produces.

1.3. Digital

Digital vs analog.

It is important to consider that this flow of information is generally carried out digitally. The term digit = finger gives us a rough idea that the representation will not take any value from the real world (analog signals) but only a discrete number of these just as the number of fingers is limited.

Bit concept

The concept of a bit is closely related to the internal representation of information. For simplicity and speed, computers store two states internally: voltage present or absent, magnetized or not, reflecting a laser or not, and so on. We represent these states mathematically as 0 and 1. This gives us the binary digit, or bit. In English, “bit” also suggests something very small; it is the smallest unit of information.

2. Communication of information

2.1. Elements of a communication system

The basic elements involved in the communication process are:

  • Sender: Entity that transmits the information.

  • Receiver: Entity that receives the information.

  • Channel: Medium by which information is transmitted, will inevitably contain noise.

  • Message: Set of signals, signs and symbols that are the object of communication.

  • Protocols and standards: Finally, it is necessary for communication to be effective that both sender and receiver follow a certain set of rules in communication. These are called protocols. Currently there are many protocols, and many of them are standardized, such as the TCP/IP protocol suite.

Thus, the transmission of information between the human being and the computer can be done in many ways:

  • Using alphanumeric characters (letters, numbers and graphic characters)

  • Through sounds

  • Through images and video

In each case it is different, and to proceed with the communication of the data it is necessary to change the way in which it is represented.

Therefore, data must always be encoded.

Optionally the data can also be encrypted, compressed and you can add redundant information for error detection and/or correction or for synchronization.

With all this, we can define information as the representation of facts, objects, values, ideas, etc., which allow communication between sender and receiver and the acquisition of knowledge about things.

As we saw at the beginning of the topic, the bit is the smallest unit to measure any information, but just as we do not measure the distance between cities in centimeters, we are not going to use the bit either, but rather multiples of it, as a general rule.

In fact, the minimum amount of information that a computer will process will be 8 bits, this is 1 Byte, and multiples thereof using Greek prefixes: Kilo, Mega, Giga, Tera, Peta, Exa, Zetta, Yotta.

Traditionally (since the 1960s) the multiplication scale has always been expressed in a power of 2 to the power of 10 (1024), although attempts have arisen to standardize the use of the scale of 1000 by the IEC since 1998, introducing the nomenclature Kibibyte, Mebibyte, etc. for powers of 2. 3.1.2. Units of measurement for the transmission of information

Traditionally, the sending of information in analogue media was carried out in baud, which measures the number of changes in the signal per second, however currently the bit per second (bps or bit/s) which is a much more precise measurement.

To name the multiples of the bit/s, the notation Kilobits/s, Megabits/s, etc. should usually be used, however it is common to find these measurements expressed in bytes per second and also in Megatransfers.

A Megatransfer (Millions of transfers per second) is equivalent to Megabit per second except for the fact that it measures all the data that is transferred including synchronization and error control information, which allows round units to be used when providing information about the transfer rate of a communications bus.

3.1.3. Image measurement units

The basic unit for measuring an image is the pixel (picture element) or point of light, whose size, color depth and distance to other points will depend entirely on the technology used and the precision that is desired. The same prefixes as for the bit are used for its multiples.

3.1.4. Conversion of storage and transmission units

Converting a measurement consists of expressing the same amount with another unit. In this section we use the decimal scale: each unit is 1000 times the one immediately below. For example, 1 kB = 1000 B, 1 MB = 1000 kB and 1 GB = 1000 MB.

  • Towards a smaller unit (down the scale): we multiply by 1000 for each jump. The numerical value increases because we need more small units to express the same quantity.
  • Towards a larger unit (going up the scale): we divide by 1000 for each jump. The numerical value decreases because we need fewer large units.
  • If there are n steps, we multiply or divide by 1000n. First identify the source and destination units and count the hops.
Storage examples
  • From GB to MB: 2.5 GB × 1000 = 2500 MB (a jump to a smaller unit).
  • From MB to GB: 750 MB ÷ 1000 = 0.75 GB (a jump to a larger unit).
  • From MB to B: 3 MB × 1000 × 1000 = 3,000,000 B (two hops: MB → kB → B).
  • From kB to GB: 2,500,000 kB ÷ 1000² = 2.5 GB (two hops: kB → MB → GB).
Transmission examples

In a transmission rate we maintain «per second» and we convert the unit of information. We use the same factors of 1000 in both bit/s and B/s. The abbreviations Mbps and Gbps are equivalent to Mbit/s and Gbit/s.

  • From Gbit/s to Mbit/s: 1.2 Gbit/s × 1000 = 1200 Mbit/s.
  • From kbit/s to Mbit/s: 50,000 kbit/s ÷ 1000 = 50 Mbit/s.
  • From MB/s to B/s: 4 MB/s × 1000² = 4,000,000 B/s.
Attention: bits and bytes are different units

b means bit and B means byte; 1 B = 8 bits. To go from bytes to bits we multiply by 8; to go from bits to bytes we divide by 8. This also applies to rates, as long as we keep the same prefix: 100 Mbit/s ÷ 8 = 12.5 MB/s, and 20 MB/s × 8 = 160 Mbit/s.

If the prefix and unit type change, we do both steps: 1 Gbit/s = 1000 Mbit/s = 125 MB/s. This equivalence expresses a theoretical rate; The useful transfer may be lower due to the overhead of the protocols and the connection conditions.

When combining storage and transmission measurements: To estimate the time, express the size and rate in compatible units and divide. A file of 500 MB to 100 Mbit/s = 12.5 MB/s ideally takes 500 ÷ 12.5 = 40 seconds.

3.2. Information representation

To choose a representation system, it is important to consider the nature of the information to be represented. The authors Alberto Prieto, Antonio Lloris and Juan Carlos Torres, in their book “Introducción a la Informática” (Introduction to Computer Science, 2006), classify itas:

  • Texts.

  • Numeric values.

  • Instructions.

  • Sounds.

  • Images and videos.

3.2.1. Numerical representation

A first approach to the problem of representing a signed n-bit number is the representation in sign and magnitude:

  • The most significant bit (MSB) indicates the sign: by convention, 0 denotes a positive number and 1 denotes a negative number;

  • The remaining (n−1) bits represent the magnitude, that is, the absolute value of the number.

A problem with this representation is the need for a conditional operation when operating with them to determine their sign and another the double representation of zero (1000000 and 0000000)

Much more used is the representation in complement notation, which allows us simpler arithmetic.

  • 1's complement (radix-minus-one complement): the zeros and ones of the magnitude are inverted — equivalent to subtraction from the radix minus one — which allows us to operate arithmetically, although the carry must be taken into account and it still has double representation for zero. It is used by the TCP, UDP, ICMP, and other protocols. e.g.: 1010)2 = -5)10

  • 2's complement (to the base): 1 is added to the 1's complement of the number, which gives us a very effective notation to operate arithmetically. It is the most used notation. e.g.: 1011)2 = -5)10

Numbers that are real or very large are usually represented using floating point notation, which is based on the property that every number n can be represented by n= m*Be where m is the mantissa, B is the base (it is not necessary to make this value explicit) and e is the exponent.

Currently, there is the standard IEEE 754 which is almost universally accepted and represents real numbers using 32 or 64 bits.

3.2.2. Alphanumeric representation (text): I/O codes

Numerical representation uses what is known as input/output encoding, which assigns each character a certain combination of bits.

According to the author J. Glenn Brookshear, in his book Introduction to Computer Science, in the 40s and 50s a very wide variety of different I/O codes began to be used, which generated the existence of communication problems, which is why they are currently used standardized I/O codes, such as:

  • BCD (Binary Coded Decimal): decimal digits are represented individually using four bits. This greatly simplifies the hardware. Variants include the Aiken code and XS3 (excess-3).

  • EBCDIC (Extended Binary Coded Decimal Interchange Code): uses eight bits to represent lowercase and uppercase letters, special symbols and control characters.

  • ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange): There are two versions, the first uses 7 data bits and 1 parity bit. The second is extended ASCII code that uses 8 bits for data.

  • UNICODE: The symbols are organized by pages, which correspond to different languages ​​or a specific set of symbols. It uses a variable number of bits (8, 16 or 32) depending on the UTF -Unicode Transformation Format- used, since in this way we can operate with less information for known codes without being limited in the number of potentially representable symbols. It is currently the most used code.

It should be added that the specific form that the letter takes will be determined by what is called “letter font”.

3.2.3. Instruction Representation

The instructions of a high-level language or assembly language are given in the form of text, represented consequently with an I/O code, and the corresponding translator, whether a compiler or an interpreter, is responsible for transforming them into machine instructions that will be represented internally as a string of bits.

3.2.4. Image and video representation

Static image encoding can be done in ways: by bitmaps (indicating its color for each bit of the screen) or by vector representation (Indicating only the vertices and color of each polygon that we want to generate).

Each pixel will be represented using an RGB (Red, Green and Blue) or CMYK (Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, BlacK -for printers) color scale, thus reducing it to three numbers (4 in the case of printing) that symbolize the amount of each color that will be mixed to generate the resulting color.

A fourth value can be incorporated into the colors on the screen that represents the transparency of the image, and which is known as the “Alpha channel”.

The resolution of an image is therefore determined by the number of points that we are going to use to represent it, but also by the number of colors that the palette that we are using allows us, according to the number of bits that we dedicate to each basic color. For example, if we use 8 bits for the palette, we can represent 28 different combinations, that is, 256 colors, but if we use 16 bits, 65536. The number of bits used is called “color depth.”

It is also common to compress images so that they take up less size, reducing the color depth, the resolution of the image, or the number of pixels to be represented by the approximating regions of bits of the same color.

A video will always be formed from static images, the number of images (frames) that are represented being important FPS and taking into account that compression can also be performed by transitions between images, that is, maintaining the elements that do not change and saving information about those that do.

3.2.4.1. Color depth

The color depth indicates how many bits are used to encode the color of each pixel. With n bits per pixel can be represented up to 2n different values. For example, 1 bit allows 2 colors and 8 bits allow 256 values, which can correspond to shades of gray or colors from a palette.

In an image RGB with 24 bits per pixel, each channel (red, green and blue) uses 8 bits: 256 levels, from 0 to 255. By combining the three channels you obtain 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 possible colors. That is why we must distinguish between bits per pixel and bits per channel.

Greater depth allows more nuances to be represented and reduces visible jumps in gradients (banding). It does not increase resolution- The resolution indicates how many pixels the image has; the depth indicates how many color values each can represent. At the same resolution and without compression, more bits per pixel require more storage space.

What about 32-bit images? In a common format, 32-bit RGBA, 24 bits are used for color and another 8 for the alpha channel (transparency). Those extra 8 bits do not increase the number of RGB colors.

4. Characteristics of a digital computer

There are a series of characteristics or parameters that allow us to compare digital computers. In this section we list the relevant characteristics of a digital computer.

4.1. Word length:

Length of the bit string that the computer can handle simultaneously (in parallel). Thus a 32-bit computer can perform operations with operands of this size in one go. Currently, 32 and 64-bit computers are sold.

4.2. Main Memory (RAM)

  • Size of the computer's main internal memory. The basic unit is the byte or octet (8 bits), although multiples are usually used.

4.3. Secondary Memory

Amount of storage or permanent memory available to the computer (usually hard drive capacity, in gigabytes or terabytes)

4.4. Bandwidth

Bandwidth is the amount of information per unit of time that can be transmitted over a bus or I/O unit. It is measured in MB/s Mb/s or GT/s

4.5. Clock frequency

The frequency at which the internal oscillator (clock) coordinates the computer’s operations, usually measured in MHz or GHz.

4.6. MIPS (Millions of Instructions per second)

Computer performance measure, which indicates the number of machine instructions that a processor can execute in one second, grouped in millions.

4.7. MFLOPS

Measurement analogous to the previous one, but exclusively for operations with real floating point numbers, which present a greater calculation load.

4.8. Synthetic tests (Benchmarks)

They are sets of varied tests whose execution times give a more reliable performance measure than MIPS, MFLOPS or clock frequency alone. Their results can be used to rank computers by performance.

5. Physical components. Von Neumann Architecture

5.1. Introduction. Von Neumann Architecture

The basic architecture model used in digital computers was established in 1945 by John Von Neumann, Hungarian physicist living in the United States.

His major contribution compared with previous architectures (mainly Harvard architecture) was to eliminate programming logic based on relays or wired connections, unifying data memory and program memory in one single main memory and thereby simplify the separate control of data and programs. This also provides flexibility in the allocation of space in memory since it can be divided between data and programs at will.

The functional elements of the Von Neumann architecture are therefore:

  • Control Unit

  • Arithmetic logic unit

  • Main Memory

  • I/O Unit

The control unit and the arithmetic-logical unit are grouped into the so-called central processing unit.

5.2. Components. CPU Functional Blocks

The central processing unit (CPU) combines the functional elements of the control unit and the arithmetic logic unit.

5.2.1. Control Unit

It is the computer’s control center, where programs are processed. Its function is to carry out the fetching, loading, sequencing and execution of the instructions.

These instructions are written in Machine Language and are made up of a opcode, which tells the control unit (CU) which operation to perform, and up to two data or memory addresses that indicate the location of that data.

To carry out its work, the UC uses the following elements:

Registers, some visible to the user and others not, among which it is worth highlighting:

  • Program counter (PC). It contains at all times the memory address of the next instruction to be executed.

  • Instruction Register (IR) Stores the current instruction, including its opcode and, where applicable, its operands or their memory addresses.

  • Memory Address Register (MAR) Stores memory addresses containing an instruction’s data or indicating where its result should be stored.

  • Memory buffer register (MIR in the Spanish notation): Any information retrieved from memory is temporarily stored.

  • Status Registers (Flags/PSW). It contains information about the result of the previous operation of the arithmetic logic unit and possible abnormal situations, such as overflows, interruptions, etc.

  • Other registers, like the general-purpose registers that can be used by the programs themselves and others special-purpose registers used by the processor (stack pointer, base register, etc) and that depend on its specific architecture.

Clock. The clock is an oscillator circuit that autonomously generates a signal in the form of pulses at constant intervals that mark the moments at which the different steps that make up each instruction must begin.

Decoder. It is responsible for extracting the operation code of the current instruction, analyzing it and emitting the necessary signals to the rest of the elements for execution through the sequencer.

Sequencer. It generates a series of very basic orders that allow the current instruction to be executed, synchronously with the system clock.

Finally, it should be noted that Control Units can be classified into two
categories:

  • Hardwired CUs. The logic is implemented in the hardware, which makes them UC of specific purpose and is not modifiable.

  • Microprogrammed CUs. In them, the logic is implemented as a program stored as firmware, allowing it to be rewritten later.

5.2.2. Arithmetic Logic Unit

It is the functional unit in charge of performing arithmetic and logical operations under the supervision of the control unit.

The operations that this unit can perform used to be very elementary, since they were normally made up of an adder-subtractor circuit, and they based all arithmetic operations on additions, decomposing complex operations into elementary steps.

Currently The Arithmetic Unit has gained great importance and the type of operations it performs is very complex, being similar to a high speed and high precision scientific calculator.

The operations that the ALU is capable of performing are classified into three groups:

  • Arithmetic. Typically they are addition (ADD), subtraction (SUB), multiplication (MUL), division (DIV) and change of sign.

  • Logical. Typically these are NOT (negation), OR (logical sum), AND (logical product) and XOR (exclusive OR).

  • Shifting. It shifts the bits of a word a specified number of positions to the right or left.

The number of operands accepted by the arithmetic logic unit also varies. Operations are classified as monadic, dyadic and triadic depending on whether they work with one, two or three data items at the same time, respectively.

5.3. Function and Purpose of Internal Memory

Memory is considered a single functional component, composed of cells that can store bits independently, with two basic operations: reading and writing.

However the limitations in terms of performance, economic cost and speed make it necessary to structure it.

5.3.1. Memory hierarchy

The author Pedro de Miguel de Anasagasti, in his book Fundamentals of Computers, characterizes memory according to:

  • The cost per bit

  • Time it takes to access information

  • Storage capacity or size

The desired values for a memory are a large storage capacity, a small access time, and a low price. However, this combination does not exist: a fast memory is an expensive memory. This limitation is valid since the first memories emerged until today. Fast memories, without refresh cycles, with almost non-existent latencies, require the use of expensive components: integrated high-conductivity flip-flops, whose manufacturing process is expensive and whose materials are expensive. From this imperative it follows that more memory capacity, more economic cost.

Fortunately not all memory needs to be accessed at any given time. This makes it possible for the instructions and data required by the processor can be organized into different levels of memory depending on how soon they need to be processed.

The memory hierarchy is then made up of very fast, expensive and low capacity memories for data currently being processed, through to relatively slow, high-capacity memories with a low cost per bit for data that is not going to be processed in the near future (not in the next few seconds). The different memories from lowest to highest capacity and highest to lowest speed and cost are classified as:

  • Registers (in the processor)

  • Cache memory (structured in levels: L1, L2, L3, etc.)

  • Main Memory (Dynamic RAM)

  • Secondary memory (Hard and Solid State Drives)

  • Auxiliary mass memory (Backup Media)

Data must be transferred from one memory to another according to the necessary availability.

5.3.2. Functions at each level

As we see in the memory hierarchy, the internal memory is made up of 3 levels. Each of which have the following specific functions:

  • Level 0 - Registers: Stores data on which the control unit is working directly.

  • Level 1 - Cache: Memory made up of SRAM integrated circuits. In turn, it is divided into several levels. It is used to minimize accesses to main memory.

  • Level 2 - Main memory. Memory made up of DRAM, whose function is to store the instructions and data that are being used in the execution of a process.

5.4. I/O buses

The set of components of a computer communicates with each other through certain paths, which constitute the interconnection structure, or I/O buses.

The Von Neumann architecture distinguishes between buses for data, address and control:

5.4.1. Data bus:

The data bus allows data exchange between the CPU and the rest of the units.

5.4.2. Address bus:

RAM memory is addressable, so each memory cell has its own address. Addresses are a number that selects a memory cell within main memory or in the address space of the input/output unit.

The address bus is a microprocessor channel completely independent of the data bus where the memory address of the data in transit is established.

The address bus consists of the set of electrical lines necessary to establish an address. The memory capacity that can be addressed depends on the number of bits that make up the address bus, being 2n the maximum size in bits of the memory bank that can be addressed with n lines. For example, to address a 256-bit memory, at least 8 lines are necessary, since 28 = 256. Additionally, control lines may be necessary to signal when the address is available on the bus. This depends on the design of the bus itself.

5.4.3. Control bus:

The control bus governs the use and access to the data and address lines. As these lines are shared by all components, certain mechanisms must be provided to control their use. Control signals transmit both commands and synchronization information, preventing information collisions in the system.

5.4.4. Multiplexed buses:

Some designs use multiplexed electrical lines for the address bus and the data bus. The same set of lines serves as the address bus at some times and the data bus at others, but never both at once. A control line indicates which function is active.

5.5. Peripherals

Peripherals connect to the I/O unit through an interface with access specifications standardized in terms of physical form, voltage and communication protocols.

Each peripheral has a controller circuit (or controller) that manages all its logic.

The peculiarities of each device are managed transparently by the operating system through a device driver software which is normally provided by the manufacturer itself.

Peripherals can be classified into Input, Output, or both input and output; the latter provide storage or communication.

6. Logical components: Software

What is Software?

Software consists of computer programs installed on devices such as computers, tablets, smartphones and smartwatches. These programs carry out specific tasks: for example, writing a letter in Word, keeping business accounts in Excel or presenting a product in PowerPoint.

Software is the intangible, logical part of a computer and includes all its applications. Software development has become a major industry in the global economy, generating millions of euros each year. Microsoft, the largest and best-known company in the field, was founded in 1975 by Bill Gates and Paul Allen. It has become a reference in software thanks to its Windows operating system and office applications such as Word, Excel and PowerPoint.

The word “software” comes from English, but the Royal Spanish Academy (RAE) has accepted its use because it is widespread among Spanish speakers. The RAE defines software as a set of programs, instructions and computer rules that enable tasks to be performed on a computer.

Software is developed using different programming languages. These languages control the behavior of a device containing a computer through symbols and syntactic and semantic rules that define its elements and expressions. They specify precisely which data the computer should operate on.

Types of Software

Software companies may define their products and categories differently, but the industry generally agrees on the following classification. It provides a straightforward introduction for people without a technical or programming background.

  1. System Software

This type is also called base software. It includes operating systems such as Windows, MacOS, Android or Linux. Computers need this software to start up and run other applications. It handles communication with the hardware, manages computer resources and configures the interface according to users’ preferences.

The classification of System Software is as follows:

  • Operating systems

  • Device Drivers

  • Diagnostic Tools

  • Correction and Optimization Tools

  • Servers/services

  • System Utilities

  • Programming Software

Alongside the operating system, another fundamental category in modern computing is programming software which makes it possible to develop other applications and systems for different tasks using programming and logical reasoning.

Programmers may write source code in a text editor and use a compiler to check it and translate it into an executable form. Today, they can also use integrated development environments (IDE) , which combine a range of programming tools in a single application.

The Classification of programming software is as follows:

  • Editors

  • Compilers

  • Interpreters

  • Linkers

  • Debuggers

  • Application programming interfaces (APIs)

  • Function libraries (library)

  • Integrated Development Environments (IDE)

  1. Application Software (Apps)

Application software is the category we use most in everyday life. With the widespread use of the Internet and smartphones, applications include the programs and utilities we use on computers, tablets and mobile phones. They are designed for end users and usually target specific operating systems.

The best-known example of this software is the Microsoft Office package or suite: Word, Excel, PowerPoint, etc. Also communication programs, games, multimedia editing, antivirus, and many others.

The classification of the application software is as follows:

  • Industrial automation and control system applications

  • Office applications (distributed in packages or suites)

  • Educational software

  • Medical software

  • Numerical computation software

  • Computer-aided design software

  • Numerical Control Software

  • Cloud applications

C. Specific software

Corporate or special-purpose applications address specific business needs, such as accounting, invoicing or tracking working hours. Examples include SAGE’s ContaPlus and Software del Sol’s FacturaSOL. Visual Studio Code, Eclipse and NetBeans are examples of software development environments.

The specific software can be further classified into:

-Professional software.

-Customer relationship systems (CRM)

-Enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems

-Decision Support Systems (DSS)

-Custom software.

Specialist professional software:

These are programs tailored to a particular profession, such as programming tools, professional audio and video editing applications, 3D engineering modeling tools (Autodesk, SketchUp), video game development tools (Unity, Unreal Engine) and web development software. 

CRM

Customer relationship management (CRM) systems, also known as front-office systems, cover a company’s sales process and follow-up, from initial marketing and the sales funnel to after-sales services. They aim to retain existing customers and acquire new ones.

A CRM is made up of at least three modules:

  • Sales module: to manage the after-sales process

  • Marketing module: for the optimization of the marketing process

  • Customer service and support module: manages hiring, services, communications, social networks, etc.

ERP

Systems for Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP), also known as back-office systems integrate modules for comprehensive business management, including invoicing, accounting, sales, purchasing, human resources, inventory and manufacturing.

DSS

Decision support information systems use the output information from ERMs and CRMs to manage decision making at the managerial level.

“Custom” software

This category includes business websites, intranets and software developed for a specific function within a company. Its specialized nature prevents general reuse and requires substantial support, training and maintenance.
