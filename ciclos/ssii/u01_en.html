1. Introduction and preliminary definitions

1.1. Hardware and Software

Computer architecture refers to the parts that make up a computer. We can distinguish two basic components:

► Physical components, collectively called hardware: all the circuitry and electronic components of the computer.

► Logical components, collectively called software: the intangible components, namely the programs that run on the computer and the information or data they process. Without software, a computer would merely be an inactive machine.

Remember:

If we can touch it, it is hardware (a physical component); if it is intangible, it is software (a logical component).

1.2. Computing and Computers

Computing is the science that studies the acquisition, representation, processing and transmission of information automatically by machines called computers.

In computing, information is a broad concept referring to a set of symbols that represent facts, objects or ideas.

A computer is a generally electronic, digital machine capable of accepting input data, performing mathematical and logical operations on it, and producing the resulting information through an output medium. It does so without human intervention by following a set of instructions—a program—previously stored in memory.

Did you know?

The term informatique was introduced in French by engineer Philippe Dreyfus in the early 1960s. It combines information and automatique.

The concept of information goes beyond that of “data”: facts needed to obtain exact knowledge or draw valid conclusions, or information arranged appropriately for computer processing.

Remember:

A computer is an electronic, digital device that processes information by following stored instructions.

Black-box model.

In systems theory, a black box receives input data, processes it and emits output information. A computer can therefore be treated as a black box when its internal operation is not relevant: it receives data and returns useful information.

1.3. Digital

Digital vs analogue.

Information flows in digital form in most computer systems. Unlike an analogue signal, which may take any value in a continuous range, a digital representation uses a discrete and limited set of values.

The bit

Computers internally store two states—voltage/no voltage, magnetised/not magnetised, reflection/no reflection—which are represented mathematically as zero and one. The term bit comes from binary digit and denotes the smallest unit of information.

2. Communicating information

2.1. Elements of a communication system

The basic elements involved in communication are:

  • Sender: the entity that transmits the information.

  • Receiver: the entity that receives it.

  • Channel: the medium through which information is transmitted; it will inevitably contain noise.

  • Message: the signals, signs and symbols being communicated.

  • Protocols and standards: shared rules that sender and receiver follow. TCP/IP is a widely standardised protocol suite.

Communication between people and computers may use alphanumeric characters, sounds, images or video. Each kind of data requires an appropriate representation.

Data must therefore always be encoded.

It may also be encrypted or compressed, and redundant information may be added for error detection, correction or synchronisation.

Information can be defined as a representation of facts, objects, values or ideas that enables communication and the acquisition of knowledge.

3. Internal representation of information

3.1. Computing units and quantities

3.1.1. Units for measuring stored information

The bit is the smallest unit of information, but in practice we normally use its multiples, just as distances between cities are not measured in centimetres.

The smallest group commonly handled as a unit is the byte: 8 bits. Larger quantities use prefixes such as kilo, mega, giga, tera, peta, exa, zetta and yotta.

Historically, multiples were often based on 210 = 1024. IEC terminology distinguishes binary prefixes such as kibibyte and mebibyte from decimal multiples based on 1000.

3.1.2. Units for measuring information transfer

Analogue transmission was traditionally measured in baud, the number of signal changes per second. Digital transfer is more precisely expressed in bits per second (bps or bit/s).

Multiples include Kbit/s, Mbit/s and Gbit/s. Rates may also be expressed in bytes per second or in transfers per second.

A megatransfer is one million transfers per second. It counts complete transfers, including any synchronisation or error-control information, rather than only useful payload bits.

3.1.3. Units for measuring images

The basic image unit is the pixel (picture element), a point whose size, colour depth and spacing depend on the technology and required precision. Pixel counts use the usual decimal prefixes.

3.2. Numeral systems

A numeral system is a set of symbols and rules: N=(S,R).

3.2.1. Positional numeral systems

Unlike non-positional systems such as Roman numerals, in a positional system a symbol's position within the number determines its value.

3.2.2. Fundamental theorem of numeration

For N={xn … x0, x-1 … x-d} in base B, its value is obtained by multiplying each digit by the corresponding power of B and adding the results.

3.2.3. The decimal system

Human beings have ten fingers; it is no coincidence that the most widely used numeral system has ten symbols, 0 to 9.

Common numeral systems include binary (base 2), decimal (base 10), and hexadecimal (base 16).

3.2.4. The binary system

Computers use binary because it is simple to represent. Its base is B=2 and its symbol set is L={0,1}.

To convert binary to decimal, apply the fundamental theorem; for example, 102 = 1·21 + 0·20 = 210.

To convert a decimal integer to binary, divide repeatedly by 2 until the quotient is less than 2, then read the final quotient and remainders in reverse order. For a fractional part, multiply repeatedly by 2 and take each integer part in order until reaching zero or the desired precision.

3.2.5. Intermediate codes

Octal (B=8, L={0,…,7}) and hexadecimal (B=16, L={0,…,9,A,…,F}) provide practical compact representations of binary information.

They convert easily to binary because each octal symbol corresponds to three bits and each hexadecimal symbol to four bits.

3.3. Representing information

A representation system must match the nature of the information: text, numeric values, instructions, sounds, images or video.

3.3.1. Numeric representation

In sign-and-magnitude representation, the most significant bit denotes the sign (0 positive, 1 negative), while the remaining n−1 bits hold the absolute magnitude. Its disadvantages include conditional arithmetic and two representations of zero.

One's complement inverts all bits; it permits arithmetic but still has two zeros. Two's complement adds one to the one's complement and is the most widely used signed-integer representation.

Floating-point notation represents real or very large numbers as n=m·Be, where m is the mantissa, B the base and e the exponent. IEEE 754 is the almost universally adopted 32-bit and 64-bit standard.

3.3.2. Alphanumeric representation: I/O codes

Input/output encoding assigns each character a particular bit pattern. Standardised encodings solved incompatibilities among earlier systems.

  • BCD represents each decimal digit with 4 bits; variants include Aiken and Excess-3.

  • EBCDIC uses 8 bits for letters, symbols and control characters.

  • ASCII originally used 7 data bits; extended variants use 8 bits.

  • Unicode organises symbols into code ranges and uses encodings such as UTF-8, UTF-16 and UTF-32. It is currently the dominant standard.

The exact visual form of a character is determined by its typeface or font.

3.3.3. Instruction representation

High-level and assembly-language instructions are written as text. A compiler or interpreter translates them into machine instructions represented internally as bit strings.

3.3.4. Image and video representation

Static images can be encoded as bitmaps, which store each pixel's colour, or as vector graphics, which describe vertices, shapes and colours.

Pixel colour is commonly represented using RGB for screens or CMYK for printing. An additional alpha channel can specify transparency.

Image quality depends on resolution and colour depth. With 8 bits, 28 = 256 values can be represented; with 16 bits, 65,536. Compression can reduce colour depth, resolution or spatial detail.

Video is a sequence of still frames. Its frame rate is measured in FPS, and compression can store differences between frames instead of repeating unchanged information.

3.3.5. Sound representation

Sound must be digitised by sampling its continuous waveform at discrete instants. Quality depends on the sampling frequency and on the number of amplitude levels, or bits per sample.

4. Characteristics of a digital computer

Several parameters allow digital computers to be compared.

4.1. Word length

The number of bits a computer can process simultaneously. A 32-bit computer can operate on 32-bit operands in one operation; common architectures use 32- or 64-bit words.

4.2. Main memory (RAM)

The capacity of the computer's primary internal memory, measured in bytes and their multiples.

4.3. Secondary storage

The computer's persistent storage capacity, usually measured in gigabytes or terabytes.

4.4. Bandwidth

The amount of information per unit of time that can travel through a bus or I/O unit, measured for example in MB/s, Mb/s or GT/s.

4.5. Clock frequency

The frequency at which the internal oscillator coordinates computer operations, generally measured in MHz or GHz.

4.6. MIPS (Millions of Instructions Per Second)

A performance measure indicating how many millions of machine instructions a processor can execute per second.

4.7. MFLOPS

A similar measure used specifically for floating-point operations, which require more computation.

4.8. Synthetic tests (benchmarks)

Standardised workloads whose execution results often compare real performance more reliably than isolated MIPS, MFLOPS or GHz figures.

5. Physical components: Von Neumann architecture

5.1. Introduction: Von Neumann architecture

The basic architecture used by digital computers was established in 1945 by John von Neumann, a Hungarian-born physicist working in the United States.

Its major contribution was to replace programming logic based on relays or wired connections and unify program and data storage in one main memory. This simplifies control and allows memory space to be allocated flexibly between programs and data.

Its functional elements are the control unit, arithmetic logic unit, main memory and I/O unit. The first two form the central processing unit (CPU).

5.2. Components: CPU functional blocks

The CPU combines the control unit and arithmetic logic unit.

5.2.1. Control unit

The control unit coordinates program processing by fetching, loading, sequencing and executing instructions.

Machine-language instructions contain an opcode, which states the operation, and up to two data items or memory addresses.

Important registers include:

  • Program counter (PC): address of the next instruction.

  • Instruction register (IR): current instruction, opcode and operands.

  • Memory address register (MAR): memory address being accessed.

  • Memory information/data register (MIR/MDR): temporarily holds data transferred to or from memory.

  • Status register (flags/PSW): operation results and exceptional conditions.

  • General-purpose and special-purpose registers: program data, stack pointer, base register and architecture-specific values.

Clock. An oscillator generates regular pulses that synchronise instruction steps.

Decoder. It extracts and interprets the opcode and sends the required signals.

Sequencer. It issues elementary, clock-synchronised orders that execute the current instruction.

Control units may be hardwired, with fixed hardware logic, or microprogrammed, with logic stored as firmware that can potentially be rewritten.

5.2.2. Arithmetic logic unit

The ALU performs arithmetic and logical operations under control-unit supervision.

Modern ALUs are complex, high-speed calculation units. Their operations include arithmetic (ADD, SUB, MUL, DIV and sign change), logic (NOT, OR, AND and XOR), and bit shifts.

Operators may be monadic, dyadic or triadic depending on whether they act on one, two or three data items.

5.3. Function and purpose of internal memory

Memory consists of independently addressable bit cells supporting two basic operations: read and write. Performance, cost and speed constraints require it to be organised as a hierarchy.

5.3.1. Memory hierarchy

Memory is characterised by cost per bit, access time and storage capacity. No technology simultaneously offers very high capacity, very low latency and very low cost: fast memory is expensive.

Because not all information is needed immediately, data is placed at different levels: fast, costly, low-capacity memory near the processor and progressively slower, cheaper, higher-capacity storage farther away.

  • Registers

  • Cache (L1, L2, L3…)

  • Main memory (DRAM)

  • Secondary storage (hard drives and solid-state drives)

  • Auxiliary mass storage (backup media)

Data is transferred between levels according to how soon it is needed.

5.3.2. Functions by level

  • Level 0 — Registers: hold data the control unit is using directly.

  • Level 1 — Cache: SRAM levels that reduce accesses to main memory.

  • Level 2 — Main memory: DRAM holding the instructions and data used by running processes.

5.4. I/O buses

Computer components communicate through interconnection paths called buses. Von Neumann systems distinguish data, address and control buses.

5.4.1. Data bus

Transfers data between the CPU and other units.

5.4.2. Address bus

Each RAM cell has an address. The address bus selects a location in main memory or I/O address space. With n address lines, up to 2n locations can be selected; for example, 8 lines distinguish 256 addresses.

5.4.3. Control bus

Carries commands and synchronisation signals that coordinate shared data and address lines and prevent collisions.

5.4.4. Multiplexed buses

Some designs reuse the same electrical lines for addresses and data at different times. A control line indicates which function is active.

5.5. Peripherals

Peripherals connect to the I/O unit through standardised interfaces that define physical form, voltage and communication protocols.

Each peripheral has a hardware controller. Its device-specific behaviour is presented transparently to the operating system by a software driver, normally supplied by the manufacturer.

Peripherals may provide input, output, both input and output, storage, or communication.

6. Logical components: Software

What is software?

Software is the collection of programs installed on computers, tablets, smartphones and other digital devices to perform tasks such as writing documents, managing accounts or creating presentations.

It is the logical, intangible equipment of a computer: applications, instructions and data. Software development is a major global industry.

Programming languages use syntactic and semantic rules to specify precisely what data a computer should process and how it should behave.

Types of software

A. System software. This includes operating systems such as Windows, macOS, Android and Linux, device drivers, diagnostic and optimisation tools, services and system utilities. It starts and manages the computer, mediates between hardware and applications and administers resources.

Programming software includes editors, compilers, interpreters, linkers, debuggers, APIs, function libraries and integrated development environments (IDEs). These tools are used to create other software.

B. Application software. Programs intended for end users, including office suites, communication tools, games, multimedia editors, antivirus products, educational and medical software, numerical calculation, computer-aided design, industrial control and cloud applications.

C. Special-purpose software. Corporate or professional applications that solve specific needs such as accounting, invoicing, time tracking, engineering, media production, game development or web development.

CRM. Customer Relationship Management systems support marketing, sales, after-sales service and customer care. Typical modules cover sales, marketing, support and customer service.

ERP. Enterprise Resource Planning systems integrate invoicing, accounting, sales, purchasing, human resources, warehousing and manufacturing.

DSS. Decision Support Systems use output from ERP and CRM systems to support management decisions.

Custom software. Websites, intranets and applications developed for a specific organisation or purpose. Their specificity entails substantial support, training and maintenance work.
